1. Research question

Among House filers in my 2025 disclosure dataset, did pairs who shared a committee report buying the same stocks more often?

2. Define the two networks

Course reading. Chapter 2 · What is a Network? and Chapter 13 · Two Mode Networks.

One network links filers to tickers they reported buying in calendar 2025. I use 3,179 accepted public-stock purchase records and count each filer–ticker tie once. The second network links members to full House committees in the November 14, 2025 roster snapshot. I matched names after normalizing spelling and reviewing name variants. Subcommittees and joint committees are outside this comparison.

Shared committee service is the social tie. I compared every possible pair within the 53-filer study group, including pairs with no shared purchases. This covers the full study group defined by the matched records.

How I built the two networks

  1. Start with 3,179 accepted House stock-purchase records from 2025 and the November 14 committee roster.
  2. Match the filers to the roster. Of 55 filers, 53 had a full committee assignment in the snapshot.
  3. Build two sets of ties for those 53 filers. One links filers to stocks they bought, and the other links them to full committees. Count each filer–stock or filer–committee tie once.

purchases <- read.csv('sna-input.csv', fileEncoding = 'UTF-8-BOM')
roster <- read.csv('work/committee-affiliations.csv', fileEncoding = 'UTF-8-BOM')
members <- sort(intersect(purchases$politician, roster$politician))
buy <- unique(subset(purchases, politician %in% members,
                     select = c(politician, ticker_v8_2_cleaned)))
assignments <- unique(subset(roster, politician %in% members))
stocks <- as.matrix(xtabs(~ politician + ticker_v8_2_cleaned, buy))
committees <- as.matrix(xtabs(~ politician + committee, assignments))
stocks <- stocks[members, , drop = FALSE]
committees <- committees[members, , drop = FALSE]
stopifnot(nrow(purchases) == 3179, length(members) == 53,
          all(purchases$asset_type == 'ST'), all(purchases$transaction_type == 'P'),
          all(purchases$ticker_parse_status == 'accepted'),
          all(grepl('^2025-', purchases$transaction_date)))
print(c(records = nrow(purchases), filers = length(unique(purchases$politician)),
        matched_filers = length(members)))
print(setdiff(unique(purchases$politician), members))

3. Compare the member projections

Course reading. Chapter 13 · Two Mode Networks and Chapter 5 · Connectivity.

I projected both networks onto the same set of members. A stock tie means two people reported at least one ticker in common. A committee tie means they shared at least one full committee. Each unordered member pair counts once. Within each committee group I calculate the percentage of pairs with a stock tie. Using percentages makes the two groups comparable even though they contain different numbers of pairs.

Result. 98 of 257 shared-committee pairs bought at least one stock in common (38.1%). Among pairs without a shared committee, 382 of 1121 did so (34.1%). The shared-committee rate was 4.1 percentage points higher.

How I compared the pairs

  1. Check each of the 1,378 distinct pairs of matched filers for at least one shared stock and at least one shared committee.
  2. Split the pairs into two groups by committee overlap. There were 257 pairs with a shared committee and 1,121 without one.
  3. Within each group, divide the number of pairs with a shared stock by the total number of pairs. That gives 98 of 257 (38.1%) and 382 of 1,121 (34.1%).
Show R code and comparison output 
overlap <- tcrossprod(stocks)
committee_overlap <- tcrossprod(committees)
pair <- upper.tri(overlap)
stock_tie <- overlap[pair] > 0
committee_tie <- committee_overlap[pair] > 0
group <- list('Shared committee' = committee_tie,
              'No shared committee' = !committee_tie)
results <- data.frame(group = names(group),
                      pairs = vapply(group, sum, integer(1)),
                      stock_ties = vapply(group, function(g) sum(g & stock_tie), integer(1)))
results$pct <- 100 * results$stock_ties / results$pairs
gap <- results$pct[1] - results$pct[2]
stopifnot(sum(results$pairs) == choose(length(members), 2),
          sum(results$stock_ties) == sum(stock_tie))
print(transform(results, pct = round(pct, 1)), row.names = FALSE)
par(mar = c(4, 10, 1, 2))
at <- barplot(rev(results$pct), names.arg = rev(results$group), horiz = TRUE,
              las = 1, xlim = c(0, 100), col = c('#8ea2b2', '#2780e3'),
              border = NA, xlab = 'Pairs with at least one shared stock (%)')
text(rev(results$pct) + 2, at, sprintf('%.1f%%', rev(results$pct)), pos = 4)

4. Interpret the result

I was curious whether House members who served on the same committees reported buying more of the same stocks. I found a modest difference: 38.1% of pairs who shared a committee reported buying at least one stock in common, compared with 34.1% of pairs who did not. That difference did not stand out to me as a strong pattern.

The comparison uses a single November 14 committee roster and purchases reported throughout 2025. Committee assignments could have changed during the year, and popular stocks or differences in how many stocks members bought could affect the overlap. Pairs can share members, and the disclosure extract is partial. The comparison describes the overlap in these records. It cannot establish that committee service influenced purchases or that members traded together.

5. Executive summary

I compared reported stock purchases across 53 House filers to see whether sharing a committee went along with buying some of the same stocks. The two groups were about four percentage points apart. What I enjoyed most was building the networks and tracing the connections back to individual filings. That process was the most interesting part of the project for me.
